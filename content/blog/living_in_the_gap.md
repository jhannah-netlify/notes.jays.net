## 2022-11-21

Wisdom / reading recommendations from my father:

**Me:** What was your book about ~Living in the Gap? Engaging in the pains of the
world even though it's uncomfortable to do so?

**Dad:** In "A Hidden Wholeness," Quaker author
[Parker Palmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parker_Palmer) writes about "living
in the tragic gap--a gap between the way things are and the way we know they
might be. It is a gap that never has been and never will be closed. If we want
to live nonviolent lives, we must learn to stand in the tragic gap, faithfully
holding the tension between reality and possibility in hopes of being opened to
a third way."

... In The Promise of Paradox he writes, "If we are willing actively to hang in
there-holding the unresolved tension between reality and possibility and
inviting something new into being- we have the chance to participate in the
evolution of a better reality." He writes about a "third way" between corrosive
cynicism and irrelevant idealism-a way that goes deeper than the intellect,
embracing the promise of paradox. I find his thoughts increasingly helpful as I
try to embrace both idealism and realism-an ongoing tension that moves me
beyond optimism, yet allows for hope. 🤩😳🥴

## 2026-07-29

*Some reflections from your Dear Ole Dad following yesterday’s phone conversation. :)*

Hi, Jay.

Your call yesterday about how to “stand in the tragic gap” between the world’s
great need and one’s personal capacities came on a day of fascinating
confluences.

I began the day hosting our weekly session of Coffee Mates: nine guys who meet
on Zoom from 7-8:30 each Tuesday morning. After our usual “How are things in
your life?” check-in, I mentioned a remark by author
[Saul Bellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henderson_the_Rain_King)
that I’d encountered the day before: *“Seek the depths or else.”*

This sparked a lively conversation. What about the counter sentiment, “Where
ignorance is bliss, ’tis folly to be wise?” Is the uncertainty of seeking
deeper meaning really worth the price of relinquishing cherished ideas? What is
the “or else” the author references when one lives a shallow life?

After the session, Bud Perrin did some research and sent me the following:

The phrase "seek the depths or else" is a dramatic warning meaning you must
actively search for profound truth, self-awareness, or spiritual understanding,
or face severe negative consequences. It combines two core ideas:  

1. Seek the Depths  
*Deep exploration:* Looking past surface-level facts, casual habits, or easy
answers to find the real truth.  
*Inner or spiritual growth:* Working hard to understand your own soul, core
values, or a higher power instead of living a shallow life.  
2. Or Else  
The warning: An ultimatum implying that staying on the surface leads to
failure, ignorance, emptiness, or a crisis

I don’t totally agree with Socrates’ dictum that “The unexamined life is not
worth living,” but I do believe that the examined life is *more* rewarding. (I’m
not so sure about the “over examined life, a tendency that sometimes befalls
me!)  :)

I shared with my mates that I have a default mechanism of “right thinking”
which I’m trying to ameliorate, but still kicks in during unguarded moments—the
childish notion that the way I view the world is THE RIGHT way. Your Mom quips
that when she married Mr. Right she had little idea how right I always thought
I was! As I’ve grown *much* more wizened I’ve also become *somewhat* wiser,
accepting the truism that “The more I know, the more I know I *don’t* know.” To
be humble, I’ve learned, is to be teachable, the opposite of know-it-all. The
discomfort of not having all the answers has been offset by the relief of
simply saying, “I don’t know.”

All these morning ruminations were in the background when I received your call
about your trip to Costco, and delivering food to Frank, a total stranger. By
coincidence Sharon and I had just had a similar request, providing a Nebraska
couple with two nights motel accommodations, lunch, and chauffeuring 120 miles
to Falls City.

My feelings sound like they may somewhat parallel your own: quite glad we
helped out, but realizing there is a much greater unmet need—a need that we may
be called upon for further help. In our case, the following day we received a
request from the Nebraska couple for additional housing, and $120 to to recover
an impounded vehicle. We declined to help further,  feeling a mixture of guilt
and “We’ve done enough.” Ambivalence.

I’m finding that I increasingly experience “dialectic tension” as I wend my way
through life: “the interplay of opposing forces or desires.” In a nod to my
Quaker brother, Parker Palmer, I’ve modified his “learn to live in the tragic
gap” to say instead “learn to live in the tragic/comic gap”—a gap so wide that
it’s wryly laughable.

Truth be told, if we’re alive we’re all culpable at some level in the world’s
woes. This is particularly difficult to acknowledge if one has the status of
white male privilege in US society. Rather than just feeling guilty, though,
I’m trying to find how best to live out a stance of “blessed to be a blessing.”

I struggle with the realization that “The ocean’s so vast, and my boat so
small.” But I refuse to let the awareness that I can’t do *everything* overcome
my effort to do *something*, be it seemingly insignificant. And maybe there’s
no such thing as “a small act of kindness.” Maybe any such acts are huge, for
the Franks, for Steves, Justins, and Tinas of our world.

I sometimes wish that I could impart to you, son, words of wisdom that would
allay the angst of becoming more fully human. I can’t impart what I haven’t
received, yet I am comforted by the inscription you gifted me years ago:

*To create order from chaos, and joy from despair — these are the imperatives of humanity.
And while none can claim perfection those that struggle onward will end their days weary —
and triumphant. Onward, my friend.*

Love always,

Dad
